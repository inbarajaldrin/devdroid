# Writing rules to copy and adapt

Treat the person's resume, supplied work and explicit corrections as evidence. The posting description and the record’s company research/summary support claims about the employer and role; they cannot supply facts about the applicant. Motivation and fit questions are the drafter’s work. Cite résumé/profile excerpts for personal claims and role/company excerpts for role claims; keep mixed claims separately scoped. An in-progress degree must never become a claim of graduation. Keep source excerpts with drafts for the reviewer.

Answer the exact question within its unit and limit. Choose one relevant proof, say what the person did and what the evidence supports, then connect it to the role's stated work. Gloss unfamiliar project names. Give each essay on one form a different purpose.

Preserve agency and scope. Built, contributed, evaluated and led mean different things. Distinguish simulation from physical deployment, prototypes from shipped products, submissions from publications, and individual from team results. Never strengthen verbs, invent teammates or attach an achievement to the wrong employer. Keep real limitations visible where relevant.

Use plain, specific sentences. Replace praise with an artifact, action or observation. Avoid generic flattery, unsupported metrics and filler that fits any application. Adapt to the person's writing preferences; do not impose another applicant's punctuation, voice, project priorities, word count, resume length or biography.

Check every assertion against its source. Could the person defend it in an interview? Keep supported claims, soften overstatements and drop inventions. Read the role's actual posting. Record sources for additional company claims in the workspace. Strip answer-file comments from text sent to forms.

Reuse approved answers for evidence and framing; adapt company-specific reasoning and preserve edits. Do not silently rewrite an approved answer or repeatedly flag settled wording. A changed fact or form can require a new review. Personal facts stay unknown until answered; a drafter may propose wording, never a nationality, visa, consent, salary or history.

Work-history text uses the correct employer, dates and role, then concise sourced actions. A why-this-team answer identifies the stated problem, one proof and concrete work the person could do there. Failure stories need actual sourced failures. Skill checklists may include supplied work beyond the resume, with source and current status recorded.
